# Reflection

The hardest decision in this project wasn't a technology choice — it was deciding, repeatedly, to treat "the team has zero experience with X" as a first-class design constraint rather than an implementation detail to work around later. That single framing shaped the Kafka decision (managed service to offset the ops burden), the database decision (Citus specifically because it extends PostgreSQL rather than replacing it), and the orchestrator decision (custom state machine over Temporal.io). Each of those, taken alone, might look like a slightly conservative choice for a "cutting-edge" system design. Taken together, they're the actual answer to the CTO's brief: a team that can genuinely operate what it ships in 15 days, not a team that has adopted every technology a textbook would recommend.

The second lesson was about evidence versus assertion. Early in the project, "Kafka can handle 12,000 TPS" or "OCC prevents write-skew" would have been true statements that no one could actually verify from the document alone. Building the shard-distribution simulator and the OCC correctness proof as real, executable code — and running them, not just describing what they'd probably show — changed the character of those two documents from claims into evidence. The measured numbers (0.52% distribution variance, the 97%-vs-3% rebalancing gap, the actual -400 overdraft the naive implementation produced) are more convincing than any amount of confident prose would have been, and they're the parts of this submission I'd defend most comfortably under hostile questioning.

The trade-off I'm least settled on is the self-hosted Citus decision from a pure operations standpoint. It's clearly the right call financially (docs/12's numbers are real) and it's the right call for keeping the team's cognitive load bounded to one database engine. But 96 self-managed EC2 instances is a genuinely large fleet for a small team to operate correctly under production pressure, and I don't think the capacity-planning document fully resolves that tension — it names the trade-off honestly rather than pretending it away, and I think that's the right level of intellectual honesty for a document like this, but it's still an open operational risk rather than a solved one.

The most useful discipline turned out to be the Day 14 consistency audit itself. I found two real problems that had been sitting in the repository for days: `docs/03`'s fraud-detection section still describing an unresolved contradiction that Day 9 had actually resolved, and a dangling ADR-007 reference pointing at a document that didn't exist yet. Neither was a large error, but both are exactly the kind of drift that a hostile reviewer — or an ARB panel member who's read the whole repository, not just the slide deck — would catch immediately. Building the central assumptions file on Day 1 made this auditable in the first place; without a single source of truth for the numbers, I don't think either inconsistency would have been findable at all, let alone fixable before submission.
